\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the test is for and where else it applies}
\label{sec:discussion-use}

A metric comparison shows whether an objective helps; the pricing test shows
what the mechanism that motivated it is worth in the metric and how much of
the objective's effect it accounts for. The test adds this decomposition, not
statistical power: because $\mathrm{MRG}_i = D_i + V_i$ exactly and $V_i$
varies little across seeds, the test of the MRG is in effect the paired test
of the FID gap against a shifted margin (Section~\ref{sec:pricing-est}), and
after Holm correction the MRG verdicts across arms coincide with those of the
FID gaps (Section~\ref{sec:worth-arms}). Its first stage, which carries most
of what the test contributes here, needs only the baseline's samples and
shows whether, with which sign and in which band the metric rewards the
statistic (P4), and how much of that is re-quantisation (P0); the second
stage needs one trained arm.

A positive MRG means that what the objective changes besides $s$ costs the
metric on balance ($D + V > 0$), not that the objective loses on net
($D > 0$), and it does not name the channel, since the MRG collects every
channel other than $s$. A negative MRG means that the objective gains more than
its change in $s$ is worth, as longer training does
(Section~\ref{sec:worth-checks}). Crediting the effect to the mechanism
requires an equivalence test: an MRG whose 90\% interval lies within
$\pm\Delta$, a margin in FID fixed before pricing, together with a value
significantly above zero. Where P4 holds, a value near zero or below means
that the mechanism cannot pay in that metric and setting.

Beyond radial spectra, the test needs a statistic of finished samples and an
operator that sets it (P2). Colour histograms, which histogram
specification sets exactly, and edge-magnitude statistics, which an unsharp
mask of chosen gain moves, admit such operators, and a metric on another
feature space can replace FID wherever it registers the statistic (P4).
Mechanisms that are properties of training, such as faster convergence or
lower gradient variance, do not qualify, because no operator on finished
samples realises them. Only the spectral instance is demonstrated here.

\subsection{Implications for frequency-aware objectives}
\label{sec:discussion-implications}

Four results bear on the design of such objectives. First, value lies where
the metric rewards it, not necessarily where the motivation places it: the
value of AWWL's change sits in radii \bandMidMin--\bandMidMax{}, the lower
part of the range AWWL up-weights, not in radii
\bandHighMin--\bandHighMax{}, mostly corner frequencies beyond the axial
Nyquist radius, where the deficit peaks (Section~\ref{sec:worth-headline}).
A band's deficit is thus a poor proxy for its value. The value side also
sets the scale before training: AWWL's whole change is worth less than the
FID gap that a five-seed paired comparison detects with 80\% power
(\mdeAWWL~FID).

Second, neither a spectral change nor a band statistic identifies a frequency
mechanism. A band weighting that depends on $\sigma_t$ is a timestep
reweighting plus a zero-sum shape term (Proposition~\ref{prop:confound}).
Min-SNR-$\gamma$, which has no frequency term, moves the unweighted high-band
mean about as much as AWWL, but only through the corner bins, and its change
is worth $\valueArmMinSNR$~FID (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}); AWWL's change
survives holding its timestep total constant and is barely produced by its
schedule alone (Section~\ref{sec:confound}), so it arises mainly from the
frequency shape. Spectral claims should therefore rest on the whole profile,
priced as a whole.

Third, the two components of such an objective cannot be judged apart. With
MSE and AWWL, the timestep-only arm and the normalisation controls cross
AWWL's schedule $S$ (varying or constant) with its frequency shape (present
or absent): each component alone is significantly worse than MSE, and
together they partly cancel (Table~\ref{tab:confound}). How AWWL's gap
divides between them depends on the order: added to the schedule, the shape
changes FID by $\dAWWLVsTimestep$ (raw and Holm $p = \dAWWLVsTimestepP$);
added to the shape at constant $S$, the schedule changes it by
$-\dSumFixedVsAWWL$ ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = \dSumFixedVsAWWLHolm$). The
crossing holds in $S$, not in $\Lambda(t)$, whose residual-dependent part
comes with the shape (Propositions~\ref{prop:confound}(b)
and~\ref{prop:residual}), so the interaction is described, not tested. A
frequency-aware objective should therefore be compared with a timestep
reweighting matched to its own total weight, not only with MSE.

Fourth, a correction delivered in full need not pay off: FFL removes nearly
all of the high-band deficit (\corrHighFFL{} of \deficitHighMSE~dB) and still
has an MRG of \mrgArmFFL~FID (Section~\ref{sec:second-member}), and on
CelebA-64, with a deficit as large as on CIFAR-10, the full match itself
raises FID (Section~\ref{sec:celeba}).

\subsection{Reporting checklist}
\label{sec:discussion-hygiene}

A report of the pricing test should state how each of the following was
handled; each of the first four changed what a result in this study appeared
to show.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Statistic, operator and achieved change.} Report $s$, the
    operator, $\delta_A$ and the change measured back in every bin (P2),
    with $s$ computed on all samples and reference images; from the first
    \num{2000} files of each listing, MSE's high-band deficit is
    \deficitHighMSEsubset{} instead of \deficitHighMSE~dB (hazard H2).
  \item \emph{Scoring without re-quantisation.} Score filtered images in
    floating point, or report the white-noise control (P0). Noise of
    $\pm0.5$~LSB lowers the baseline's FID by \priceNoiseHalfValue{} on
    CIFAR-10 and \celebaNoiseHalfValue{} on CelebA-64, but does not lower
    that of held-out CIFAR-10 test images (value $\realNoiseHalfValue$; one
    measurement), and at $\pm1$~LSB it raises it ($\realNoiseOneValue$), so
    the hazard belongs to the operating point; it does not bias comparisons
    between trained arms, which are quantised alike.
  \item \emph{Sampler.} Measure $s$ and $M$ on the same samples and name the
    sampler (P1): AWWL's high-band change is \corrHighAWWL~dB under DDIM-100
    and $\corrHighAWWLddpm$~dB under DDPM-1000 (Section~\ref{sec:sampler}).
  \item \emph{Paired estimates.} Report $D$, $V$ and the MRG with paired
    intervals, raw and Holm-adjusted $p$ within a declared family, the
    smallest resolvable effect, and open estimator choices (the sign of
    AWWL's KID gap to MSE depends on the KID implementation;
    Appendix~\ref{app:supp-kid}).
  \item \emph{Controls and sensitivity.} Run a positive and a null control, a
    second operator (P3) and the no-interaction check (P5), and report the
    value of the training-made change below which the cost outside the change
    would lose its positive interval.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Recipe and resolution.}
The significant MRG verdicts on trained objectives come from one small
recipe: CIFAR-10 at
$32\times32$, a U-Net whose only self-attention layer is in the bottleneck, no
dropout, 200 epochs without weight averaging, DDIM-100 and FID from
\num{10000} samples. Its baseline FID, \fidMSEmean, is far from the state of
the art; MSE reaches \longBestFIDMSE{} at epoch \longBestEpochMSE{} of the
800-epoch schedule with weight averaging, where three seeds resolve no MRG,
at that optimum ($\mrgLongOpt$ $[\mrgLongOptLo, \mrgLongOptHi]$) or at epoch
799 ($\mrgLong$ $[\mrgLongLo, \mrgLongHi]$). The pricing is also a statement
about DDIM sampling, since under DDPM-1000 AWWL does not raise the high
band. AWWL was not trained on CelebA-64, so no MRG exists outside
CIFAR-10, and whether AWWL pays at $64\times64$, where
\citet{chandran2026spectral} report small gains for related losses, is open.
The design does not separate the causes of the sign change of the price on
CelebA-64: data, resolution, operating point and number of optimiser steps
(Section~\ref{sec:celeba}).

\paragraph{Sample size and the across-arm claim.}
With five seeds, the paired AWWL--MSE test has 80\% power against a true
difference of \mdeAWWL~FID, and the observed gap is not significant. AWWL's
MRG (raw $p = \mrgMainP$) does not survive Holm correction across the
\nFamilyPricing{} priced arms ($p = \mrgArmAWWLHolm$), and in KID its sign
holds without significance ($\kidMrgMain$
$[\kidMrgMainLo, \kidMrgMainHi] \times 10^{-3}$, $p = \kidMrgMainP$). The
claim that frequency-aware objectives fall short of the value of their
spectral change therefore rests on the arms that survive correction
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pricing-arms}): FFL \citep{jiang2021focal}, trained without
the spatial loss its authors add, and our own variants of AWWL, not a
sample of the family; AWWL + EMA (three seeds) and the null control do not
survive. $L_1$, Charbonnier and Min-SNR-$\gamma$, without a frequency term,
also have significant positive MRGs.

\paragraph{Operators and the identifying assumption.}
Every pricing operator is a linear radial gain, which leaves phase, anisotropy
and per-image variation unchanged, so effects through them fall into the MRG;
FFL, whose weights act on the full two-dimensional spectrum, is the most
exposed arm. The value, and with it the MRG, depends on the operator (P3): white
noise is worth far more per decibel than a coherent gain, and the boost gives
$\mathrm{MRG} = \mrgBoost$ $[\mrgBoostLo, \mrgBoostHi]$. The identifying
assumption, that a change made by a filter is valued like the same change made
by training, was tested for one change, AWWL's, by planting it in the training
data ($\mathrm{MRG} = \mrgPlanted$ $[\mrgPlantedLo, +\mrgPlantedHi]$;
Section~\ref{sec:worth-planted}); for other changes it remains an assumption,
and the positive and null controls, which return
$\mathrm{MRG} \approx D$ when $|V|$ is small, check only the direction and the
accounting. The sensitivity analysis bounds its effect instead: the cost
outside the change, $D + V_{\mathrm{train}}$, keeps a positive uncorrected
interval as long as the training-made change is worth
$V_{\mathrm{train}} > \tipValue$~FID, \tipShare\% of the filter's value
(Section~\ref{sec:worth-checks}); after Holm correction AWWL's MRG is
unresolved even at $V_{\mathrm{train}} = V$, so the analysis bounds the
uncorrected verdict only. For the
transplant, the interaction (P5) is small and not resolved: removing AWWL's
change from its own samples costs \priceReverseValue~FID, against
\priceTransplantValue{} for adding it to MSE's (paired difference \interactionTransplant{}
$[\interactionTransplantLo, \interactionTransplantHi]$,
$p = \interactionTransplantP$).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
FID and KID compare Inception-V3 features and inherit their priorities
\citep{kynkaanniemi2023role,stein2023exposing,jayasumana2024rethinking}. A
value in FID is not a demonstrated gain in perceived quality, as the response
to sub-LSB noise shows. The main measurements were repeated in DINOv2
features (Section~\ref{sec:worth-dino}), where the verdict on AWWL is stronger
and hazard H1 does not appear; the remaining values, and the band and arm
detail of Section~\ref{sec:worth-arms}, are Inception-specific, and no human
study was run. The DreamBooth metrics (CLIP
ViT-B/32 scores, one subject, no DINO similarity) do not register the
statistic (P4).

\paragraph{The audited objective is our own.}
AWWL was introduced by the present authors, with two further co-authors, in the proceedings of CITA 2026
\citep{thao2026awwl}, which creates a risk of bias in either direction. The
Holm families are declared in the code and reported with raw $p$
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-stats}), and every number is generated from the run
ledgers by scripts released with them (Data availability), so each can be
checked independently.
