Objectives built around a mechanism, such as frequency-aware diffusion losses,
are judged by comparing trained models, which shows whether they help, not
what the mechanism is worth. Our pricing test imposes a trained
objective's change in an output statistic on baseline samples: the metric
improvement is the change's value $V$, and the mechanism residual gap (MRG),
the objective's metric minus the filtered baseline's, equals $D + V$ for the
observed gap $D$. Controls check the verdict's sign, and a planted-mechanism control its
identifying assumption. On CIFAR-10, no
configuration has a significantly lower Fr\'echet Inception Distance (FID)
than the mean-squared-error baseline. The spectral change of Adaptive Weight
Wavelet Loss (AWWL), introduced in our conference paper, is worth
\priceTransplantValue{}~FID, mostly at mid radial frequencies rather than
where the deficit peaks. AWWL's MRG is \mrgMain{}~FID (raw
$p = \mrgMainP$, Holm $p = \mrgArmAWWLHolm$ over \nFamilyPricing{}
configurations). Trained alone, its timestep schedule and, at constant total
weight, its frequency shape each raise FID significantly ($+\dTimestep$,
$+\dSumFixed$) and partly cancel when combined; the shape produces most of
the spectral change. Across configurations, corrected MRG and FID-gap
verdicts coincide: the test adds a decomposition, not statistical power.
White noise within half an 8-bit step lowers the baseline's FID by
\priceNoiseHalfValue{}, though not that of held-out real images, so
re-quantising filtered samples overvalues them. In DINOv2 features AWWL's gap
is resolved, its change is worth nothing, and noise raises the distance. On CelebA-64, correcting a high-frequency
deficit of the same size raises FID.
