\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A frequency-aware diffusion objective can do exactly what it was designed to
do --- measurably correcting a real spectral deficiency, through the component
its design credits --- and still lose to the baseline it was meant to improve
on. That is the result, and the interesting part is not the loss but the
distance: the correction is worth $\boostValue \pm \boostCI$~FID, the objective
delivers it, and it ends \shortfall~FID short of its own prediction. A
twenty-line post-process obtaining the same correction for free outperforms it.

Getting to that number required treating the mechanism as a quantity rather
than a story. It is not enough to show that a method changes what it targets;
the change has to be priced against what the evaluation metric can register,
and against what the method gave up to obtain it. On an orthonormal basis
Eq.~\ref{eq:parseval} makes that trade explicit --- sub-band weighting
redistributes a fixed error budget, so every gain is someone else's loss --- and
the question is never whether the mechanism is real but whether the trade is
favourable.

The pricing test is what we would most like to see used elsewhere. Any
objective whose stated mechanism is a change to an image statistic admits it:
apply that change to the baseline's outputs directly and see what it is worth.
If the trained method does not clear the post-processed baseline, the
training-time machinery is not paying for itself, whatever the ablations show.
It requires no additional training, and it would have flagged the objective
audited here before any of the sweeps in \S\ref{sec:results} were run.

Two results temper how much any of this should be trusted on its own. The
weighting we audit turns out not to isolate the axis it names: its frequency
parameter simultaneously imposes a timestep schedule correlated with Min-SNR at
$|r| = 0.82$, and on DreamBooth the only objective whose effect survives
correction is a pure timestep method with no frequency component at all. And
both measurement hazards we report --- a bespoke KID estimator that inverted the
sign of the study's only positive result, and a calibration performed at the
metric's minimum that understated its operating-point sensitivity roughly
thirtyfold --- were caught only because we asked what a result \emph{should}
have been before accepting what it was. Neither would have announced itself.

We do not conclude that frequency-aware objectives cannot work. We conclude
that one of them does not, that the reason is legible once the mechanism is
priced, and that the pricing is cheap enough there is no good reason not to do
it.
